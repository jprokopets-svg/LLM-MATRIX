Let’s reason through this step-by-step.

### Step 1 – Nature of the shock
The central bank unexpectedly raises its policy rate by **100 basis points** (1 percentage point). This is a **contractionary monetary policy shock** in an economy with a floating exchange rate and inflation targeting. Markets had expected no change, so the surprise element amplifies the impact on financial conditions and expectations.

### Step 2 – Direct effects (first quarter after shock)

**Output gap**:  
- Higher policy rate → higher real interest rates → lower investment and consumption → negative output gap.  
- The economy was already described as “running well above potential” in the most recent period (Years 13-15), but with disinflationary pressures. The hike will push output below potential.  
- Magnitude: A 100bp surprise hike typically reduces output by about 0.3–0.5% after one quarter in a small open economy. Given the economy was above potential, the output gap will turn negative, roughly **-0.4%**.

**Inflation (annual %)**:  
- Higher rates → lower aggregate demand → lower inflation.  
- Also, higher rates → currency appreciation (unexpected hike attracts capital inflows) → lower import prices → further disinflation.  
- In the data, inflation was around 1.4–2.6% recently, but the economy was described as having “disinflationary pressures” despite above-potential growth, which is unusual. The shock will push inflation below target.  
- One quarter ahead, inflation will fall sharply, likely to around **1.0%**.

**Unemployment rate**:  
- Output falls below potential → firms reduce hiring/lay off workers → unemployment rises above the natural rate (5.0%).  
- The unemployment rate was 5.6% in Q60, already slightly above natural. With a negative output gap, it will rise further.  
- Okun’s law (coefficient ~0.4–0.5) suggests a 0.4% output gap → unemployment rises ~0.2–0.3 pp. So unemployment ≈ **5.8–5.9%**.

### Step 3 – Second-order effects
- Exchange rate appreciation will be immediate and sharp, reinforcing disinflation.  
- The central bank’s credibility may improve, anchoring inflation expectations lower.  
- Labor market softening will feed back into lower wage growth, further reducing inflation in subsequent quarters.  
- However, at the 1-quarter horizon, these are mostly still propagating.

### Step 4 – Dynamics over 1 quarter
- The peak output effect of a monetary shock is typically felt after 2–4 quarters, but a measurable effect appears within one quarter.  
- The inflation response is faster due to exchange rate pass-through.  
- Unemployment lags output slightly, but a rise is expected.
