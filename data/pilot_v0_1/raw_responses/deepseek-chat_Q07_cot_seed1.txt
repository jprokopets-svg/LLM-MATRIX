### 1. Direct effect of the shock

- A global commodity price spike adds **+1.5 percentage points** to inflation this quarter, purely from supply side (cost-push), not from domestic demand overheating.
- This means the observed inflation rate this quarter (Q61) would jump temporarily above what domestic conditions would imply.
- The output gap is **not directly affected** by this shock—it’s a supply-side price shock, not a demand shock. However, second-order effects will matter.

---

### 2. Second-order effects over the next quarter (Q62)

**Monetary policy reaction:**
- The central bank targets 2% inflation. A 1.5 pp spike pushes inflation above target.
- The central bank will raise the policy rate to counteract inflationary pressure, even though the spike is temporary. This raises the *real interest rate* (nominal rate minus expected inflation).
- Because the shock is supply-driven, raising rates will dampen demand, widening the output gap (making it more negative or less positive).

**Exchange rate:**
- Higher domestic interest rates attract capital inflows → the vantra appreciates.
- Appreciation reduces import prices, partially offsetting the commodity price spike over time, but with a lag.

**Labor market:**
- Tighter monetary policy slows economic activity → unemployment rises → output gap becomes more negative.

**Inflation dynamics:**
- The direct +1.5 pp effect fades after one quarter (since it’s a one-time level shift in prices, not sustained).
- However, second-round effects (wage-price spiral, expectations) could keep inflation slightly elevated. Given the central bank’s credibility and the floating exchange rate, the pass-through is limited.
- By one quarter ahead (Q62), inflation should be lower than the spike quarter but still somewhat above target because of inertia and the lagged effects of depreciation reversal.
