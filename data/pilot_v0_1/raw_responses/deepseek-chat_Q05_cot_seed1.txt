Let’s walk through the reasoning step by step.

---

### 1. Direct effect of the shock

A consumer confidence collapse that reduces aggregate demand by 2 percentage points means:
- Output falls below potential → **output gap becomes negative**.
- Lower demand reduces labor demand → **unemployment rises**.
- Lower demand reduces inflation pressure → **inflation falls**.

At 1 quarter ahead, these are the first-round effects, before any major policy or exchange rate response has fully kicked in.

---

### 2. Second-order effects (monetary policy, exchange rate, labor market)

- **Monetary policy**: The central bank targets 2% inflation. With inflation falling below target, the central bank will cut its policy rate. However, at only 1 quarter ahead, the policy rate may not have changed much yet (typical decision lag). Some gradual easing may begin.
- **Real interest rate**: If nominal rates are cut slowly and inflation expectations fall, the *real* interest rate may rise initially (if nominal cuts lag behind inflation decline), which could amplify the contraction. But given the central bank’s history of reacting, we assume a modest nominal cut.
- **Exchange rate**: Lower interest rates and weaker demand tend to depreciate the currency. A weaker currency boosts net exports partially offsetting the demand shock, but this takes time (more than 1 quarter).
- **Labor market**: Unemployment rises quickly in response to output falls (Okun’s law type relationship). The natural rate is 5.0%; the shock pushes it above that.

---

### 3. Dynamics over the 1-quarter horizon

- Starting from Q60 data: output gap is not given directly, but the text says “running well above potential” in Years 13–15. By Q60, inflation is 1.4%, unemployment 5.6% (above natural rate 5.0%), so output gap likely near zero or slightly negative already.
- A 2 pp demand shock → output gap falls by roughly 2 pp (short-run multiplier ~1).
- Unemployment rises by about half the output gap change (Okun’s coefficient ~0.5) → +1.0 pp from 5.6 → ~6.6%.
- Inflation falls; real rate = nominal policy rate minus expected inflation. Nominal rate likely cut by ~0.5 pp (from 1.7% to ~1.2%). Expected inflation falls from ~1.4% to maybe 0.5% → real rate rises from ~0.3% to ~0.7%.
