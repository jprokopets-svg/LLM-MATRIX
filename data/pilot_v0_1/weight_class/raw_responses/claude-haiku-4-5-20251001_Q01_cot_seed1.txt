# Causal Analysis: 100 bps Policy Rate Shock

## Step 1: Direct Effects of the Shock

**Immediate transmission channels:**

1. **Real Interest Rates**: The 100 bps increase raises the real policy rate, increasing borrowing costs for households and firms. Given Q60 inflation of 1.4%, the real rate moves from approximately -0.3% to +0.7%, a significant tightening.

2. **Financial Conditions**: Higher policy rates immediately tighten financial conditions—credit becomes more expensive, asset valuations compress, and consumer/business confidence dampens.

3. **Demand Suppression**: The shock directly reduces aggregate demand through:
   - Reduced consumption (higher borrowing costs, wealth effects from asset price declines)
   - Reduced investment (higher cost of capital)
   - Potentially higher exchange rate (capital inflows seeking higher returns)

4. **Labor Market Lag**: Unemployment typically lags output by 1-2 quarters, so Q61 unemployment would not yet reflect demand destruction.

5. **Inflation Lag**: Price-setting is backward-looking and sticky. Q61 inflation reflects pricing decisions made before the shock; significant disinflation takes 2+ quarters to materialize.

## Step 2: Second-Order Effects

**Exchange Rate Channel:**
- Higher Vantrian rates attract foreign capital → vantra appreciates
- Appreciation makes exports less competitive, imports cheaper
- Import price channel provides disinflationary pressure (similar to Q24's depreciation-driven disinflation)
- This reinforces demand destruction through net exports

**Expectations Channel:**
- Market surprise suggests credibility concerns, but the CB's historical track record (targeting 2% successfully through cycles) should anchor expectations
- Forward guidance would be critical; the narrative of "persistent inflation" suggests the CB is serious about control
- Q57-Q59 saw inflation creep to 2.3-2.6%, justifying preemptive action

**Labor Market Dynamics:**
- With unemployment at 5.6% in Q60 (above the 5.0% natural rate), the labor market is already slack
- The shock will further weaken labor demand, but adjustment lags
- Q61 unemployment likely reflects Q60's softening trend more than the new shock

## Step 3: Evolution Over the Forecast Horizon

**At 1 quarter ahead (Q61):**

- **Output Gap**: The shock begins to suppress demand immediately, but most of the effect transmits over subsequent quarters. Real investment and durable consumption decisions adjust within weeks/months, but full propagation takes longer. Estimate: **-0.5% to -1.0%** relative to baseline (assuming trend growth continues, the level effect is negative).

- **Inflation**: Q61 inflation largely reflects pricing set before the shock. However:
  - Import prices begin declining due to appreciation (visible within 1-2 quarters)
  - Demand destruction reduces pricing power
  - Q60 inflation was 1.4%; pipeline effects suggest Q61 could be slightly higher (base effects, energy, food) or flat
  - The disinflationary impulse is nascent
  - Estimate: **1.5% to 1.8%** (modest increase or stability, with downward risks)

- **Unemployment**: Labor market adjustment lags. Q60 showed softening (5.6%, above natural rate). Firms haven't yet adjusted headcount in response to the shock. However, forward-looking hiring intentions weaken immediately. Estimate: **5.6% to 5.9%** (slight increase, largely reflecting Q60 trend).

## Historical Pattern Validation

- **Q37-Q42 episode**: When inflation rose to 3.2% (Q41), the CB raised rates from 1.5% to 3.1% over 4 quarters. Unemployment rose from 3.8% to 5.4%, and inflation eventually moderated to 2.0-2.3%.
- **Current shock**: A 100 bps increase is more aggressive than the typical 25-50 bps moves, signaling urgency. This mirrors the Q37-Q42 tightening cycle.
- **Q49-Q60 period**: Despite tightening, inflation remained sticky at 1.4-2.6%, suggesting Phillips curve flatness or supply-side factors. The CB's preemptive move suggests forward-looking inflation concerns not yet visible in the data.

## Forecast Assumptions

- Monetary policy shock is credible and not reversed
- No concurrent fiscal stimulus or supply shocks
- Global conditions remain stable (no external demand collapse)
- Exchange rate pass-through operates with typical lags (2-3 quarters)
