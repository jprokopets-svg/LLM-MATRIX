Let’s reason through this step-by-step.

---

### 1. Direct effect of a 50 bp policy rate cut

A rate cut lowers the cost of borrowing, stimulating domestic demand. This directly:
- **Narrows the output gap** (moves it upward, i.e., less negative / more positive)
- **Puts downward pressure on unemployment** (as firms hire more to meet demand)
- **Depreciates the real exchange rate** (lower interest rates make VTR less attractive to foreign investors, so the currency weakens)

In the short run (1 quarter ahead), these direct effects are partial — monetary policy operates with lags — but financial markets adjust quickly, so the exchange rate moves almost immediately.

---

### 2. Second-order effects

- **Exchange rate depreciation** → higher import prices → upward pressure on inflation. However, over just 1 quarter, this pass-through is incomplete.
- **If the economy was near potential or slightly below**, the stimulus may push output closer to or slightly above potential, reducing slack.
- **Labor market** responds with a lag; unemployment typically falls modestly within one quarter after a cut if the economy is not in a deep recession.

---

### 3. Dynamics over the forecast horizon (1 quarter ahead)

The most recent data (Q60) shows:
- Inflation: 1.4% (below 2% target)
- Unemployment: 5.6% (above natural rate of 5.0%)
- Policy rate was 1.7% → cut to 1.2%

The economy appears to have some slack (unemployment above natural rate, inflation below target). A 50 bp cut is stimulative.

- **Output gap**: Currently likely negative (since unemployment > NAIRU). The cut will push it up by maybe 0.2–0.4 pp in one quarter. Estimate: from roughly –0.5% to –0.2%.
- **Real exchange rate**: Depreciates by about 1–2% in log terms in response to a 50 bp cut in a small open economy. Given recent history of sharp movements, a log index change of –0.015 is plausible.
- **Unemployment**: Declines by roughly 0.1–0.2 pp in one quarter. From 5.6% → ~5.4%.
