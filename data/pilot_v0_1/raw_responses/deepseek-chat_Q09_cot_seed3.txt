Let’s reason through this step by step.

### 1. Direct effects of a sudden 5% nominal depreciation

- **Real exchange rate (log index):**  
  A nominal depreciation of 5% directly lowers the real exchange rate (makes domestic goods cheaper relative to foreign) by roughly the same amount in the short run, assuming prices are sticky. If the log index is normalized so that a higher number = appreciation, then a 5% depreciation means a drop of about 0.05 log points.  
  *Point estimate:* If we assume the pre-shock log real exchange rate was around 0 (neutral), then after shock it’s approximately -0.05.

- **Inflation (annual %):**  
  A depreciation raises import prices, feeding directly into consumer prices. Pass-through is typically partial within one quarter. For a small open economy, a 5% depreciation might raise CPI inflation by about 0.5–1.0 percentage points in the first quarter (assuming ~10–20% pass-through).  
  *Point estimate:* If inflation was near target (~2.0%) before the shock, it could rise to ~2.6–2.8%.

- **Output gap (%):**  
  Depreciation boosts net exports (cheaper exports, more expensive imports shift demand to domestic goods), so output rises above potential. The first-quarter effect is modest but positive, say +0.3 to +0.5% of potential GDP.  
  *Point estimate:* If output gap was near zero, it becomes slightly positive, e.g., +0.4%.

---

### 2. Second-order effects

- **Monetary policy reaction:**  
  The central bank targets 2% inflation. With inflation rising above target, they would likely raise the policy rate. However, they might wait one quarter to confirm the shock is persistent. At 1 quarter ahead, the policy rate may not yet have changed, so the inflation and output effects are still from the direct shock.

- **Exchange rate dynamics:**  
  A risk-off episode often leads to a sharp depreciation, but if the central bank does not immediately hike, the currency may stabilize or even slightly recover as markets price in future tightening. However, at 1 quarter, the depreciation mostly sticks.

- **Labor market:**  
  Higher output reduces unemployment with a lag. At 1 quarter, the unemployment rate may start to fall, reinforcing the positive output gap.

---

### 3. Dynamics over the forecast horizon (1 quarter ahead)

- **Real exchange rate:** Still near the depreciated level, maybe a very slight recovery (0.01 log point).  
- **Inflation:** Peaking in the quarter of the shock due to import price pass-through.  
- **Output gap:** Positive but modest; the full trade response takes longer than one quarter.

---
